## Title of project

*Uncertain Field Notes*

## Description of project / research

*(473 words, limit 500)*

How do you represent a place that no longer exists?

This question emerges throughout my entire art practice. My grandmother was resettled from Pinchard's Island, one of hundreds of outport communities in Newfoundland, Canada, emptied by government relocation programs in the mid twentieth century. The island is still there, but of the community only a graveyard remains. What survives is intangible heritage: dialect in the Dictionary of Newfoundland English, Memorial University's archives, and my family's oral history.

From those fragments I am building a machine that tries to represent a lost place and fails honestly: a solar powered field rover that holds a camera and a language model. It navigates on its own, photographs what it finds, and tries to describe what it sees. Working only from archival fragments rather than fluent knowledge, it misnames things, contradicts itself, and admits where the words are gone, testing where the resilience of the oral tradition finally fails. The gap between the photographs and the faltering field notes is the work: the failure of a living heritage to pass between generations, made visible rather than smoothed over.

This approach runs against the grain of the technology it uses. To make machines sound fluent about everything, artificial intelligence companies digitize libraries as raw material, often cutting books apart and destroying them. Where that industry treats culture as something to own, I build the opposite: a field rover working only from what little survived of one place, something held and safeguarded rather than possessed, whose failures stay in view. I intend to create an open system others can extend, so Newfoundlanders, scholars, and artists contribute language and memory to a living shared record, never finished.

The rover is deliberately provisional. I am developing it as a ladder of prototypes, from a low cost tracked unit I can test now toward a rugged, self charging version built to work unattended. My intention is to leave a later version alone on Pinchard's Island across the seasons, so the record is kept by a guest on the land: present, recording, without belonging. To realize this rover, I am drawing on significant experience with cameras and microelectronics in an image-based and installation practice, and years of building software systems. That path continues earlier works: a live solar camera on my family's cabin (Cloudberry), a cabin rebuilt around that feed (Cabin), and a sculpture driven by live Atlantic data (Driftwood).

To Artica, I would bring a working prototype to test the parts that must survive winter unattended, the camera, solar power, and cold endurance. I would also test the writing: how a Newfoundland inheritance meets High Arctic ground: a stress test of mismatch, not a search for Arctic training data. Over four weeks the outcomes would be tested hardware, image and text experiments, and a clearer path toward the Pinchard's Island deployment.

## Why is it necessary that your residency is in Svalbard?

*(199 words, limit 300)*

This project is about speaking for a place without fully belonging there. Svalbard holds that tension as fact rather than metaphor: what remains when extraction ends meets the pressure of incomplete belonging as a structural condition of life. I want to evolve the work inside that pressure, rather than think it through from a distance.

Svalbard communities are made and unmade by migration and by whether what built them still sustains them; few remain for a whole life. Longyearbyen was built for coal and is developing alternative reasons to continue. Svea was demolished and returned to tundra once extraction ended, until only protected pre-1946 traces remained. My family's outport was emptied by resettlement when its life was judged no longer viable, until only a graveyard remained: forced displacement, lived rather than imagined. My work on Pinchard's Island explores a fourth path: attending to a place after that way of living is gone. 

This residency belongs in Longyearbyen, among a community choosing to continue. Cold and darkness I can test elsewhere; a community's continuation after extraction, and incomplete belonging as lived condition, I cannot. There I can also engage the ethics this work raises: bringing a machine and camera into a heavily governed land with a long extractive history means asking who may occupy and represent land, under what permissions, and what it means to leave rather than extract. Artica gives time to test, collaborate, and stay with those tensions while working with consent, within the rules, and toward community dialogue.

Practically, I would time the stay to the edge of returning light at the end of the polar night, to test how the device wakes when light returns after no power, and what a guest may say of a place it can barely see, without claiming local belonging.

I would host an artist talk at Nordover, or the Longyearbyen Library if that fits better. The talk would situate earlier works that carried a resettled Newfoundland island into other spaces, followed by the current machine: one that photographs what it finds and writes uncertain field notes from fragments.

At the end of the residency, I would leave copies of photographs and notes with Artica, and with the Longyearbyen Library if useful, so a record remains available here to keep reading, refusing, or reshaping after the rover leaves.
