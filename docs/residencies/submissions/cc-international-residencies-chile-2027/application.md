## Describe your proposed activities

How do you represent a place that no longer exists?

What survives of Pinchard's Island is intangible heritage: the Dictionary of Newfoundland English, published historical resettlement records, and my family's oral history. From those fragments I am building a machine that tries to represent that place and keeps the failure in view.

I have been selected for La Wayaka Current's Desert 23°S arts and ecology residency near San Pedro de Atacama, Chile, October 23 to November 13, 2027. I am asking the Canada Council for the Arts to support three weeks of creative research, creation, and international exchange.

THE PROJECT

I speak a Newfoundland dialect that contains unique, place-based words for weather, for shoreline, for the behaviour of ice. I have handed elements of that heritage to a camera system that has yet to stand where the words were formed. These words, and the camera's fumbling with them, are part of what is left.

That fumbling is the subject of Uncertain Field Notes: a solar-powered field rover for Pinchard's Island, a resettled outport on Newfoundland (Ktaqmkuk), where my grandmother was born and the place my work keeps returning to. The island is still there; the living community is not. The rover will photograph as it explores and write field notes about what it sees, trying to understand a place through language it never fully inherited. Working from fragments, it misnames, contradicts itself, asks rather than answers, and refuses to speak when speech would invent fluent local knowledge it lacks. The artwork is the gap between photograph and uncertain field note: the failure of a living heritage to pass cleanly between generations, kept visible rather than smoothed over.

Today the project is a small physical kit in the studio: a microcomputer (Raspberry Pi) with a camera module and a limited language model. No chassis, no wheels yet. The next step is to test it in the field. In the Atacama Desert I will set the system down outdoors, in front of the other artists. In early 2028 I will run a winter field test in Newfoundland with a wheeled rover, and in spring 2028, with consent and site access in place, the rover will be deployed on Pinchard's Island as an ongoing but maintained presence, fully removable, so nothing is physically left behind.

The work grows from a practice of carrying Pinchard's Island across distance: a solar camera on my family cabin (Cloudberry), an installation that rebuilt a room around the remote feed (Cabin), and a sculpture driven by live Atlantic Ocean data (Driftwood). With Cloudberry I have already run a solar camera outdoors, unattended, on that cabin. Uncertain Field Notes adds what these projects never had: a language model that attempts to speak.

HOW THE NOTES ARE WRITTEN

On the device, a small vision model looks at the photograph, and a general-purpose language model writes the field note. What the model says is limited to an initial corpus I assemble with permission: the Dictionary of Newfoundland English, accessed through Memorial's Digital Archives Initiative; selected published historical resettlement records; and my family's oral history of Pinchard's Island. A dictionary describes a language without being one. An archive holds what was recorded, not what was lived. Oral history is what somebody chose to tell. When the corpus has no word for what the camera sees, the note asks, stalls, or goes quiet. It does not invent a fluent note about a place it cannot name. Instead it asks: What do you call a shore with no boat? I will ask again tomorrow.

This approach runs against the grain of the technology it uses. To make machines sound fluent about everything and refuse almost nothing, artificial intelligence companies digitise libraries at industrial scale, and in documented cases buy books, cut the spines off, scan the pages, and discard the originals. Culture as inventory to own. The Newfoundland material does not teach this model. It restricts. The field notes are written by a machine that could say anything, held to the few words one place left behind, safeguarded rather than owned.

I have included an example of this method from my studio as the last audiovisual file (10-uncertain-field-notes-sample.jpg), titled Uncertain Field Notes (working sample): a pre-existing photograph of Pinchard's Island beside a note limited to the cited sources. This field note is a demonstration, not a finished work.

WHY THIS RESIDENCY

La Wayaka Current's Desert 23°S gives independent research time, guided field visits, sessions with the host's long-term local collaborators, and other artists who can sit with a photograph beside an uncertain note. It puts three parts of the work on ground I cannot reach in the studio: how the machine is present, who may shape what it says, and whether its writing survives a place it has no words for.

A machine that looks and writes raises unsettling questions of presence. The Atacama around San Pedro is inhabited, governed, and culturally held land, including Lickanantay / Atacameño territory, and it sits under heavy extractive pressure, its salt flats mined for lithium. The critique the machine carries, against culture stripped and owned at scale, meets a landscape being stripped for the batteries that power machines like it. As a guest there the machine is limited in what it may say: a temporary presence, speech that can defer, ask, or withhold. It takes nothing from the ground it stands on: solar, un-anchored, fully removable. Held to the host's protocols, it does not photograph where permission is refused, does not make the Atacama its cultural subject, does not claim a partnership it does not have, and does not treat local speech as corpus material. On site this becomes an ethics conversation on presence and a camera on inhabited land. This is not only a Chile concern: Pinchard's Island is also Indigenous land, owed the same care.

The corpus behind the limited language model is assembled with permission, but who may change it, and how, is an ethical question I cannot settle alone. Because the words come from a community that is gone, the rules for revising them carry weight, and they need testing by artists who make and govern work on inhabited, culturally held land. During the residency I will host a workshop on site to draft and pressure-test a consent-and-data protocol: who may revise the corpus, and how, when, and why. Their critique shapes the working draft; its direction belongs with other Newfoundlanders, the people who hold the dialect.

The language also has to meet a landscape it has no words for. On a Newfoundland coast the corpus already describes, the notes can hide inside familiarity. The Atacama provides that pressure: a terrain the Newfoundland lexicon was never written for. The camera captures a dry landscape with a hard horizon. The note reaches for fog, for frost, for pack ice, and tries to describe this ground in words made for another. That visible gap is the research: a stress test of mismatch, not a search for local training material. The daily test builds one pair after another, a desert photograph beside a faltering Newfoundland-constrained note, critiqued at an open studio and presented at the host's public sharing in San Pedro.

Over these three weeks the outcomes are a kit tested in heat and dust, a set of desert photographs beside faltering notes, and a clearer specification for the 2028 winterized build and the deployment on Pinchard's Island.

## How will this project develop and enhance your international profile and that of Canadian artists or arts practices?

I am a Montréal-based media artist working from my family's Newfoundland resettlement history via photography, live data, and microelectronics. My previous work has lived close to home; Desert 23°S takes it out of that comfort, into an international arts-and-ecology cohort and the wider debate on AI and cultural extraction. What raises my standing is bringing that debate into an international cohort with work that does not smooth over failure: a permission-based machine willing to refuse, read by peers who did not grow up with the dialect. The practice earns recognition beyond Newfoundland and Canada.

## How will this project build international connections and exchange knowledge?

The exchange is built into the residency's structure: an international arts-and-ecology cohort, sessions with the host's long-term local collaborators, and a public sharing in San Pedro. What I bring is an ethics conversation on guest presence, a peer workshop that sets photographs and their field notes beside a draft consent-and-data protocol, and an open studio. The questions I put on the table are ones I cannot answer alone: who may revise a language corpus, and how a guest machine should speak. Peer critique from artists across cultures and ecologies, and guidance from local collaborators about creating on inhabited land, carry back into how the rover behaves as a guest.

## How will this project generate future opportunities?

This residency unlocks the next stages of the project. A consent-and-data protocol pressure-tested outside my own community provides a pathway to later evolve the corpus in collaboration with organizations such as Memorial University's Folklore and Language Archive and the Centre for Newfoundland Studies. My intent is to invite other Newfoundlanders, scholars, and artists to contribute language, memory, and artifacts to the corpus. The desert research and source materials strengthen funding applications for the full 2028 rover build and deployment on Pinchard's Island.

## Project timeline

BEFORE RESIDENCY (not funded via this grant)
The camera system has produced notes from photographs in Montréal. What remains before departure is device testing, packing existing kit, and coordinating field visits with the host.

WEEK 1
Arrival, orientation, first programmed field visits, and introductions to the local collaborators. I set the daily routine: photograph, generate field notes on the device, and record where it refuses and why. I also review solar yield, battery, and connectivity. The first outdoor pairs are made, each a desert photograph beside a Newfoundland-constrained dialect note. An ethics roundtable with the other artists opens questions of guest presence and a camera on inhabited land, looking for pathways rather than a finished policy.

Milestones: the first outdoor photographs and field note pairs, baseline power and coverage readings, and the ethics conversation.

WEEK 2
Programmed field visits continue alongside self-directed time. I grow the set of pairs across varied terrain, distance, altitude, and light. I document how the language fails: where the corpus reaches for the wrong thing, where it stalls, where a refusal reads as evasion instead of honesty. I may adjust prompting and refusal thresholds, but I do not change the corpus alone. Mid-week, a workshop with the other artists tests a draft consent-and-data protocol against their critique.

Milestones: a larger, more varied set of pairs and the themes, an account of where the language fails, observations of how the kit holds up with sustained heat and dust, and the workshop.

WEEK 3
Final captures, followed by curating the image and field note pairs. I log solar yield and battery states, record thermal behaviour and dust ingress. The week closes with an open studio with the other artists and a presentation at the host's public sharing in San Pedro, where I place the residency work inside my wider practice and show the pairs as a photograph beside a faltering note. The sharing puts the pairs in front of an audience with no tie to the dialect, the first test of whether the notes hold for people outside the community that made them.

Milestones: a curated subset of pairs, the open studio and public sharing, and method notes for the return to Montréal.

Three weeks of desert data allows me to determine the power budget, enclosure and duty-cycle specification for the 2028 winterized build, which is the part of the rover I cannot specify from studio testing alone.

AFTER RESIDENCY (not funded via this grant)
I synthesize the pairs and method notes in Montréal and present the work to the Post Image Cluster at Milieux, where I am an active member. I submit later funding applications for the full build.

Supports
The host's fixed programme keeps the work on schedule and provides accommodation, field visits, local transport, and peer and team support; I work independently within it.
